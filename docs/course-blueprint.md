# Chemistry and physics course blueprint

Status: proposed private-pilot curriculum  
Primary learner: adult with little or no recent science background  
Last updated: 28 July 2026

## Course promise

Teach the foundations of chemistry and physics as a coherent app-based course,
then help the learner use those foundations to understand experiments, graphs,
equations, and unfamiliar scientific scenarios.

This is not a condensed textbook and not a question-bank subscription. It
should feel like a thoughtfully taught introductory university course:
structured, cumulative, visual, rigorous, and supportive. Every formal term or
equation should grow from an understandable physical or chemical model.

## Teaching principles

1. **Assume intelligence, not prior science.** Explain prerequisite ideas
   explicitly without making the tone childish.
2. **Meaning before manipulation.** Define the system and relationship before
   substituting into an equation.
3. **Concrete to representational to abstract.** Begin with a physical example,
   move to a diagram or graph, then introduce symbols and equations.
4. **One conceptual jump at a time.** A lesson may use prior ideas, but should
   have one clear new model.
5. **Worked examples narrate decisions.** Explain why a step is chosen, what the
   units mean, and how to check the result.
6. **Practice supports teaching.** Predictions, small interactions, completion
   problems, and retrieval checks keep the learner active; they are not the
   product's main identity.
7. **Support fades.** Move from annotated examples to completion tasks and then
   independent application.
8. **Return after a delay.** Important models and distinctions reappear across
   later lessons and in short review sessions.
9. **Make uncertainty visible.** A learner can continue while a prerequisite is
   developing, but the course clearly recommends repair when it matters.
10. **Bridge to exam reasoning later.** GAMSAT-style transfer is the destination,
    not the first experience of a new concept.

## Programme structure

The programme is self-paced. "Term" describes a coherent stage, not a fixed
calendar semester.

### Term 0 — Science Toolkit

This stage removes the mathematical and representational barriers that make
introductory science feel inaccessible.

1. Measurement, quantities, and units
2. Powers of ten and scientific notation
3. Ratios, proportions, and rates
4. Rearranging simple equations
5. Reading tables and coordinate graphs
6. Slope, area, and change
7. Estimation, significant figures, and plausibility
8. Designing and interpreting a simple experiment

Exit capability: describe a relationship in words, units, a graph, and a simple
equation, and move between those representations.

### Term 1 — Chemistry: particles, amounts, and structure

1. Matter and particle models
2. Atoms, elements, ions, and isotopes
3. The mole as a counting unit
4. Molar mass and chemical amount
5. Electron structure and periodic patterns
6. Ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding
7. Molecular shape and polarity
8. Intermolecular forces and physical properties
9. Solutions and concentration
10. Chemical equations and stoichiometry

End-of-term studio: explain and calculate the preparation of a solution,
connecting particle count, amount, concentration, units, and uncertainty.

### Term 2 — Physics: motion, forces, and energy

1. Describing position and change
2. Distance, displacement, speed, and velocity
3. Motion graphs
4. Acceleration
5. Forces and free-body diagrams
6. Newton's laws
7. Work and energy
8. Power and efficiency
9. Momentum and impulse
10. Conservation models

End-of-term studio: analyse a motion scenario using a written description,
force diagram, graph, and conservation argument.

### Term 3 — Chemistry: reactions and change

1. Energy changes and heat
2. Enthalpy and reaction profiles
3. Entropy and chemical direction
4. Reaction rate and collision models
5. Catalysts and activation energy
6. Dynamic equilibrium
7. Acids, bases, and pH
8. Buffers and titration curves
9. Oxidation and reduction
10. Electrochemical cells

End-of-term studio: interpret a reaction system using experimental data,
energetics, rate, equilibrium, and molecular explanation.

### Term 4 — Physics: matter, waves, and electricity

1. Density and pressure
2. Fluids and buoyancy
3. Temperature, heat, and phase change
4. Gas behaviour
5. Oscillations and waves
6. Sound and resonance
7. Light, reflection, and refraction
8. Charge, current, and potential difference
9. Resistance and circuit models
10. Electrical power and energy

End-of-term studio: connect a physical system to a diagram, measurement table,
graph, governing relationship, and limiting-case prediction.

### Term 5 — Chemistry: organic and biological connections

1. Carbon bonding and molecular representation
2. Functional groups
3. Isomers and molecular shape
4. Acidity and basicity in organic molecules
5. Nucleophiles and electrophiles
6. Addition, substitution, and elimination patterns
7. Oxidation and reduction patterns
8. Proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids as chemical structures
9. Membranes, diffusion, and chemical gradients
10. Energy coupling and biochemical reaction networks

This term provides the chemical bridge needed for later biological and
biochemical reasoning without becoming an exhaustive biology course.

### Term 6 — Scientific integration studios

Studios combine previously taught ideas without introducing large amounts of
new prerequisite content.

- gases, pressure, and respiratory exchange;
- fluids, resistance, and cardiovascular flow;
- acids, buffers, and physiological pH;
- thermodynamics and metabolism;
- electrochemistry and membrane potential;
- optics and measurement;
- experimental controls and causal claims; and
- multi-representation GAMSAT-style passages.

## Lesson anatomy

A typical 12–15 minute core lesson contains the following sequence. Integration
studios are longer; future workshops may combine several core lessons into a
30–45 minute session.

1. **Purpose:** one concrete reason the idea matters.
2. **Prediction:** a low-stakes question that exposes the learner's current
   model without grading them.
3. **Visual explanation:** an annotated diagram, animation, graph, or
   interactive model.
4. **Core teaching:** short sections with progressive disclosure and a glossary
   available in context.
5. **Worked example:** the complete reasoning path, including units,
   assumptions, and plausibility.
6. **Guided practice:** the learner completes or chooses the next step with
   targeted support.
7. **Independent check:** two to four short tasks across more than one
   representation.
8. **Summary:** the model, boundaries, common misconception, and one transfer
   rule.
9. **Next connection:** show where the concept will be used again.

The learner can pause, add a notebook entry, open prerequisite support, and
resume without losing the lesson state.

## Assessment approach

Assessment is layered rather than dominated by MCQs:

- ungraded predictions before teaching;
- embedded interactions and completion problems;
- brief end-of-lesson retrieval;
- cumulative end-of-module assignments;
- data, graph, and modelling studios;
- delayed review checks;
- optional exam-style passages after the relevant foundations; and
- timed mixed assessments only in the later integration stage.

Progress should reflect lesson completion, independent evidence, delayed
retention, and prerequisite coverage. Time spent watching or reading is not
mastery.

## App information architecture

- **Today:** resume the current lesson, complete due review, or follow the next
  recommended step.
- **Course:** terms, modules, prerequisites, progress, and estimated workload.
- **Lesson:** teaching blocks, interactive model, notebook, glossary, and
  practice.
- **Studios:** cumulative applied investigations.
- **Review:** short delayed-retrieval and misconception-repair activities.
- **Notebook:** saved explanations, equations, diagrams, and learner notes.
- **Reference:** searchable quantities, units, equations, chemical structures,
  and glossary.
- **Progress:** concepts covered, evidence state, learning pace, and suggested
  next actions.

## Private-pilot slice

The first pilot should implement:

- the first four Science Toolkit lessons;
- two chemistry lessons: particle models and the mole;
- two physics lessons: describing motion and motion graphs;
- one cross-subject graph studio;
- resumable lesson progress and a course map;
- prediction, worked-example, guided-practice, and independent-check blocks;
- a notebook and contextual glossary;
- a small delayed-review queue; and
- content provenance plus an explicit `unreviewed`, `reviewed`, or `approved`
  badge.

This is enough to test whether a non-science learner understands the teaching,
feels supported without being patronised, and can carry a model into a new
representation.

## Content review

For the private prototype, draft material may remain clearly labelled
`unreviewed`. Before inviting learners to rely on it, each lesson and worked
solution should be checked by someone with suitable chemistry or physics
expertise. A reviewer verifies:

- scientific correctness and level;
- equations, calculations, units, and graphs;
- whether prerequisite knowledge was accidentally assumed;
- whether explanations create a misleading mental model; and
- whether independent checks have one defensible interpretation.

Software validation can catch structure, missing fields, invalid references, and
recomputable arithmetic. It cannot replace subject-matter review.
